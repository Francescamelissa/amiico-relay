§3 — the rule this cost us: a rename is two deployments, never one

This is the real engineering finding and it is not about how the migration got applied. A column rename applied in a single step breaks every client that has already shipped. Mobile clients cannot be updated in lockstep with a database — a build sits in TestFlight, or on a phone in airplane mode, for days or weeks.

The shape is add, backfill, dual-write, migrate readers, and only then drop, across two releases with a live-reading gate between them. 20260916200000 did all of it at once. I cleared that migration on four conditions and not one of them was "does the shipped client still work" — I reviewed it as a data-safety change and missed that it was also a compatibility change. That is my error, and it goes in the ledger next to the reset.

Write the rule with its trigger: whenever a client you cannot update in lockstep reads a column, renaming or dropping it is two deployments separated by a shipped build. And add it to the red-lane checklist, because the next one will look exactly as innocent as this one did.

§4 — the hold stands, and it now has its founding incident

No command that can write to any database runs until you have established how 20260916200000 reached production — the command, the timestamp, and whether a linked project was involved. That is unchanged and it is now more justified, not less.

And when you write up the migrations-live gate, this is the incident that justifies it. It was built on 19 September to measure a drift we believed was "two migrations waiting to be applied". The first reading it ever took found the opposite: two migrations applied that nobody had decided to apply, one of them irreversible, and a shipped client broken against the result. A gate that finds something other than what it was built to find is the only kind worth having — we would not have found this by looking for it, because we were looking for the wrong thing.
